## Minimum success criterion

- lower 95% bound on saving per request: \$0.000000; net over 100,000 requests after the audit: \$-0.64
- **Successful switching**: not met (needs tolerance met, positive net savings, better cost per success)
- **Successful abstention**: MET (needs fewer harmful switches than current CASE, and no worse than the raw log within uncertainty)

Current CASE makes 0.20 harmful switches per run; the safe protocol makes 0.00 and abstains on 2.98 of three decisions.

**Verdict.** The safe audit prevented a model switch that the simpler CASE protocol would have made.

## What actually improved, and what did not

**Inverse-variance combination is the one clear methodological win.** Replacing the audit-overwrite
step with an inverse-variance combination of the audit estimate and the routing-adjusted logged
estimate cuts harmful switches from 0.20 to 0.08 per run, a 60% reduction, while reaching the best
held-out success of any arm (0.445). This costs nothing to adopt and needs no new data.

**Richer corrections did not help much on this workload.** Additive, partially pooled and low-rank
corrections all land within noise of the one-adjustment baseline on success, and low-rank is worse.
With four models and three domains there are only twelve pairs and about eight are bought, so there is
not enough data for an interaction term to be estimated well. The partially pooled model is the right
shape; this workload is too small to show it.

**Full expected-value acquisition was the worst arm** (0.389 success at \$35.37 per 1k). Its priority
multiplies several estimated quantities, and on twelve pairs that product is dominated by noise.

**The abstention is near-total, not selective.** The safe protocol abstains on 2.98 of three
decisions. It meets the abstention criterion because it avoids the harmful switches, but with 59 tasks
the 95% intervals are wide enough that almost nothing clears the bar. That is a statement about
evidence volume, not about the rule discriminating well.

## Comparability note

The selective log here is generated by a cheapest-model rule with 0.15 exploration, not the fitted
router used in the paper's RQ5 replay, so absolute levels differ from that table (raw log 0.316 here
against 0.487 there). The arms in this file are internally comparable to each other; they are not
directly comparable to the RQ5 numbers. Under this harsher log, the current CASE protocol does raise
success (0.316 to 0.437), which is the opposite of what it does under the fitted-router log. That
sensitivity to the log-generating process is itself worth recording.

## Scope

This is a public-benchmark protocol result. It does not establish deployment readiness, which
additionally requires prospective traffic, stable model versions, validated operational
outcomes and a canary or shadow deployment.
